## What This Piece Is

The pre-expo essay. Publishes on SL **before October 18** to build traction for The Aisle and establish the "ministry without a pulpit" thesis. Ends in the present tense — *"on October 18 we find out"* — not a retrospective. The reader participates in the outcome.

This is NOT the full memoir essay. That comes after the expo when the arc is complete (Bloom Bar as centerpiece, Oak Meadow in the room). This piece builds to the threshold. The memoir essay crosses it.

**Thesis:** The pulpit was one form. The Aisle, Southern Legends, the farmers market, the Bloom Bar — all the same call, different vessels. Ministry doesn't end when the institution does. It finds new forms.

**The line the whole piece hangs on:**
> "You can be in ministry without working at a church."

## Section 3 — The Object *(scaffold drafted — Matt edits)*

We bought it while I was manic.

May 2025. $7,000. A converted horse trailer from a couple in Orlando — it used to be a hat bar in Florida. They bought it from the hat bar people with plans to make it a drinks bar, never got around to it, let it sit covered in their backyard for years. We bought it on a hope and a prayer. My son and I drove to Orlando to pick it up, sunburned and proud. We turned it into a Bloom Bar — a floral installation, white and elegant, the kind of centerpiece you see at wedding expos in Nashville. We used it for one spring.

Then the flower farm closed.

My father-in-law moved the Bloom Bar to the yard while I was in the hospital. It sat in the grass between our trailer and his house. I saw it every day when I came home.

She was politely told no. She drove home. What did she look like when she got home? What did she say, if anything? You don't need to editorialize. Report what happened.

*Scaffold bones (Matt edits):*

Heather drove to Oak Meadow Event Center in Ohatchee in June, when she was trying to grow the floristry to support us, just as the picture of the financial strain was becoming clear. She brought them an arrangement she'd made from flowers we'd grown. They gave her a tour. Then they politely told her they don't give referrals to florists they haven't worked with. She drove home deflated.

We kept the Bloom Bar anyway. We didn't know why.

## Section 5 — What I'm Building *(scaffold drafted — Matt edits)*

The Aisle opens October 18 at Anniston Museums and Gardens — a curated bridal expo, East Alabama's first at a cultural institution. Photographers, florists, cake artists, caterers, live music, alpacas, a champagne welcome.

One section is called "The Whole of It." Michelle and Ray Hornsby lost their son Justin to suicide in 2017. Michelle planted a willow tree. Then she and Ray built a healing farm on that same land — 120 animals, a therapy alpaca named Justine, free comfort sessions for anyone carrying grief. Their mission: *if you want to heal, go help someone.* They'll be there on October 18.

We set aside free tickets for every show — no questions asked — for couples who need them. Price should never be the reason someone misses this.

## Section 7 — The Close *(Matt writes)*

*Prompt:* This is the ending. The memoir essay ends with the Bloom Bar as the centerpiece on October 18. This essay ends before that — at the threshold. You don't know yet if it works. Write from there.

The Bloom Bar line is yours — use it or hold it:
> The Bloom Bar didn't sit there doing nothing. It was waiting.

Then: what are *you* waiting for? Not sentimentally — specifically. What does October 18 mean to you, in one sentence? Not the hope. The stakes.

Then the Lucy line. Her exact words. Let them close the door.

*One option for the very last line — not "I'm starting to believe her." That's Foothills. This essay deserves something that opens outward instead of closes down. What do you actually think now? Or: silence. Let her line be the last sound in the room.*
