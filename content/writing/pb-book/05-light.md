Around January I started to find it within me.

I had spent weeks trying to figure out how to undo what I had done. Quitting the church. The money. Sinking the farm. We sold the farm. We had to. A lot happened after that. I had been in the hospital. I had come out into a camper at my parents'. I had moved into the trailer in Pleasant Valley after Thanksgiving. I had spent Christmas there. Our first.

The therapy was twice a week at first.

I did not have a job. I was applying for jobs. The credit cards were maxed. The retirement loan was carrying us week to week. Heather had started at the museum the same week I went into the hospital and was still inside the first ninety days of it.

I had stopped going to church entirely.

In the mornings I started listening to *Pray As You Go*, the Jesuit podcast, while I walked. The stories did not always land. They still do not always. But the muscle memory carried me.

The kids came home from school. We made dinner. I lit a candle at bedtime and we sang the song we had always sung — *walk in the light, the beautiful light*. The candle was the constant. Through the manic year. Through the hospital. Through Cropwell. The same song. The same candle. I had not given that up because I did not want to give up on the kids.

It was the smallest thing I had left, and it was the thing that held.

---

The wilderness was not just a metaphor.

I went on long walks on Little John Road in Pleasant Valley. Past farms and gardens and fences and houses. Things people had that I had. That I had built. That I had been planning to build.

I still hesitated before looking at the farms. I looked at them anyway.

A friend of mine has had bipolar since her twenties. She put it best when she said: *Yes, my serotonin is store-bought.*

I had spent decades thinking faith and willpower could carry me through anything. The first depression in 2024 had broken that belief. The second depression in 2025 broke whatever I had built back. Accepting the medication was not the surrender. The surrender was accepting that the version of me that needed to be the one with the answers was over.

I had a degree in pastoral counseling. I had fifteen years in a pulpit. I had built a flower farm and a SaaS suite and a marketing campaign for a bicentennial that was seven years out. None of it had saved me.

The medication did. And therapy. And the candle.

---

In ministry I had preached a verse for fifteen years.

*Unless a kernel of wheat falls to the ground and dies, it remains only a single seed. But if it dies, it produces many seeds.*

I had said it from pulpits. I had used it in funeral homilies. I had quoted it in coffee shops to people in transition. I had not understood it.

The seed does not survive and then bear fruit. It dies and *therefore* bears fruit. Loss is not the detour around the life you wanted. It is the path into the life that becomes possible. The grain of wheat keeps dying.

That is not tragedy. That is the pattern.

The farm had to die. The pulpit had to die. The version of me that built the SaaS suite at four in the morning had to die. None of those deaths were what I wanted. All of them were the path.

The framework I am writing about in this book starts here. Not in the manic peak. Not in the hospital. Here, on Little John Road in Pleasant Valley, walking past farms I no longer owned, with store-bought serotonin in my system and the muscle memory of a Jesuit podcast in my ears.

The clarity I teach now is what was left after the cleverness was burned off.
