## Opening

August 2025. I was sitting in the parking lot of Jack's, a quarter mile from the house.

I had not ordered anything. I was just in the car with the engine running. The radio was off. I sat there a long time watching cars go by.

Heather was inside the house with Annie Brunson, the realtor. They were walking the property. The high tunnel. The flower field. The kiosk on the trail. The little white and green farmhouse we had bought in 2021. Annie was a friend from the farmers market, a flower patron for years. She had agreed to list it at cost.

Heather had asked me to leave for a couple of hours. I could not be there for it.

I sat with the engine on and thought about whether there was any way to turn it around.

---

I had been trying to fix it for months.

After the resignation I doubled down on the farm. Then the cash flow caught up. We started meeting with our accountant.

Heather said it in the office. *"We're desperate. We need help."*

We started meeting with my aunt, retired financial adviser. She helped us see a plan. The plan kept showing the same thing. The cash flow was not there.

That was August.

I started chasing jobs. I asked the church for my position back. The answer kept being no. I worked construction with Preston, Heather's brother, carrying six-by-sixes twelve feet long. Painted a deck for a month in Jacksonville. When that ran out I took manual labor at Camp Lee — the church-owned camp — driving the buggy, cutting brush, building a low ropes course, hoping the camp work would put me in front of leadership and turn something around.

The whole time I was trying to fix the thing the farm could not afford to keep.

The offer came in. The house was sold. I was in the hospital by then.

>
> We parked, walked to UAB. They waited with me in the ER for six hours. The psychiatrist asked if I wanted to be admitted to the residential program. "I do not." I left with a spring in my step.
>
> The next day, I called Dad to take me back.

---

I was in the psych center for ten days. I told people, and myself, that it had been three weeks. I was sure, until I checked the journal.

The notebook was cheap, brown, lined paper. I wrote with the golf pencil they gave me. Less dangerous than a full length one. Sterile hospital air. The Indian girl that was kind, also bipolar. Stellar carrot cake. I learned from the others that you had to write it in on the meal card, though they occasionally got the meals wrong.

The ones desperate to leave, yelling and crying on the phone. The schizophrenic guy talking to imaginary people. The ones alone in their rooms down the hall, laughing and carrying on conversations. I remember all of them.

I journaled, *I used to run, strength train, bike to work and write sermons.* Feeling trapped, walking in circles. The shower dribbling water, huddling in the corner to get wet. The yoga mat calling me. Snack time, pb crackers, oreos. Stepping out at 1am for sleep meds and pb crackers.

---

A couple of mornings a week they brought us individually into Dr. Bhatia's office. She sat in the center of the room, the large window to the hospital courtyard behind her, surrounded by three training psychiatrists and the social worker. Big table in a small room. Looking in her eyes across the table.

*"It sounds like you've tried a lot of different medications. That's bad practice. Cycling through them before reaching maximum therapeutic effect."*

She was talking about my psychiatrist. I did not know who was right.

---

Heather took all my calls while I was in there. She started her new job at the museum, while packing boxes, selling furniture on marketplace, homeschooling the kids. She visited me three times during my ten days. Once to see me. Once for the notary, house closure papers, my signature. Walking out of the ward, with Heather and the social worker to meet the notary, felt like freedom. A third time because the notary had taken the day off, so we had to reschedule and hire a mobile notary. We quietly signed papers and stamped our thumbs.

---

Sam, a woman with a hijab, took me to a quiet room. She asked questions.

*"You had to move as a child because of choices your dad made. Now the same thing you've worked so hard to avoid is happening to you and your family. This is a major trigger for you — the thought of losing the farm is triggering your depression."*

That had never occurred to me. That connection. Not sure it helped, but something in it rang truer than any other bs I'd heard that week.
