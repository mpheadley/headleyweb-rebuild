In May 2025, Ciara Smith-Roston visited the farm.

She was the mayor of Anniston. She came on what was supposed to be an official walk-through of the Chief Ladiga Trail extension that ran behind our property. We had just connected the farm to the trail. We had the power line approved. The Bloom Bar — the seven-thousand-dollar custom floral installation we had bought a year earlier and used once — was set up on the property. The walk-in cooler was running. The dahlias and peonies were hitting their third-year prime.

When she arrived, she told us to call her Sierra.

She bent down and gave my daughter Sage a hug. Then she invited Sage in for one. She came as a person, not as an official.

We gave Sierra flowers from Heather's field. She stood in the field while Heather and I shared our vision for the Chief Ladiga Trail Porch — the trail shop we were going to build, the bicentennial campaign for 2032, the bridge across the trail to the Walmart lot. She looked out and said:

*"This is so peaceful out here. I can see it. Y'all's vision is beautiful and powerful."*

I took it as confirmation.

---

Four months before Sierra's visit, I had quit my job.

I resigned from First Methodist Anniston the Thursday after Ash Wednesday. The decision arrived with the confidence of calling. I sent the resignation by text. I dumped everything I had been holding for twenty years of ministry into an exit document I wrote with ChatGPT in the front seat of my car — the bait-and-switch hire, the denied raise, the demotion to staff music director, the way the senior pastor handled the resignation conversation. I was furious. I was clear. I was wrong about how clear I was.

Looking back I'm pretty sure I was in AI psychosis by then.

That morning we sat the kids down in the living room and told them. They cried. They asked if it meant we were leaving the church.

We said no.

(We did leave, months later. They were right to ask.)

---

Between the resignation in February and the hospital in October, I built more than I had built in the previous five years combined. Thirty SaaS apps in a weekend. A new brand for the farm — Wholehearted Homestead — built out as a "Ladiga Trail Porch" trailhead concept with full marketing materials and a hand-drawn logo. A flower farm at maximum scale. A book project I called *The AI Mystic*. A Patreon. A bridal expo concept. A multi-year campaign for the 2032 bicentennial of the Treaty of Cusseta and Chief Ladiga's signing.

I was waking up at four in the morning. I was sleeping three hours a night.

I was running thirty-minute errands that took eight hours.

I fell asleep at the wheel on a ten-minute drive between the church and home.

I had a gold star in a doctor's office in March.

I had gone in to Dr. Hadley feeling better. I was pumped. I had something good to report. The depression I had walked through in 2024 was lifting. I was excited to tell her.

She smiled. She said: *you will get better.* Then she added that we might want a prescription for the sleep — magnesium, or Seroquel, I do not remember which. I disregarded it. I did not fill it. I forgot it happened.

That was the warning I missed.

When I came back months later, after the farm was being sold and I was coming down hard, she remembered. She said she had used the word *manic* in that earlier visit. We had not used the word *bipolar* yet. She said we should have.

The gold star was the trap door.

---

Jay Jenkins kept buying flowers.

He was a ward 1 councilman, an architect, a class of '83 Donoho graduate. He had been a weekly customer at Heather's farmers market booth for four years. He had hired Heather for his daughter's wedding the year before — her first wedding contract. He drove past our booth every Saturday morning that the manic version of me was livestreaming and missing the kids' faces.

He did not know what was happening behind the booth.

He just kept showing up.
